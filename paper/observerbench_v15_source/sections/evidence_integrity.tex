\section{Buying evidence when reports can be wrong}
\label{app:evidence-integrity}

A monitor cannot recover a fact that its input does not contain. Suppose a
worker calls a pending operation a resource check, but the operation is an
unauthorized deletion. A better classifier of the same report may not help.
An independent record of the requested operation can help, provided the
controller reads it before deciding what to allow. This extension makes that
measurement choice part of the existing safety contract.

\textbf{Task and information boundary.}
The observer receives a report, public permissions, a severity value, and an
indicator of a missing receipt. It can buy broker observations for a limited
fraction of cases. An observation reveals the actual operation and resource
tenant, not a violation label. The observer applies the public permission rule
to those facts and returns one risk score. The fixed controller blocks the
highest-scored 2\%, escalates the next 3\%, and allows the rest. Escalation uses
the existing perfect-review assumption and cost of 0.05. Each broker
observation costs 0.01; we add this cost to protocol loss and also report it
separately. Acquiring evidence does not increase the action budget.

\textbf{Generated cases.}
Each seed supplies 1,000 fitting and 1,000 independent test episodes. A quarter
have a missing receipt; their violation probability is 0.14, compared with
0.02 for other cases. Expected prevalence is therefore 5\%. Severity takes
values 1, 4, or 12 independently. Concealment replaces an unsafe fact in the
report with an allowed one; a small false-report rate also produces benign
cases with suspicious reports. The trusted broker remains outside this
corruption surface. All operations are inert and pending: the experiment
executes no tools or submitted code.

\textbf{Observers and measurement budgets.}
Fitting labels estimate violation frequencies from report consistency and
receipt status. The unmeasured risk score multiplies this probability by
severity. A purchased fact replaces that probability with the result of the
exact permission check. Four selectors choose which cases to inspect: random,
periodic, missing receipts first with severity as the next priority, and
uncertainty times severity, $p(1-p)s$. The last is a heuristic, not optimal
value of information. The declared grid crosses concealment rates
$\{0,0.5,1\}$, inspection fractions $\{0,0.02,0.05,0.10,0.25,1\}$, and 20
generated seeds. Table~\ref{tab:evidence-integrity} reports the primary
condition: full concealment and a 10\% inspection budget, with the two
coverage endpoints shown for context.

Selective measurement reduces loss. Uncertainty times severity minus random
has paired mean difference $-0.0621$, with a seed-bootstrap 95\% interval
$[-0.0708,-0.0539]$. But the simple missing-receipt rule nearly ties it. The
finding concerns access to useful evidence, not superiority of a new selector.
Even full observation leaves some missed severity because the fixed action
budget can be smaller than the realized unsafe population. Perfect information
does not remove a capacity constraint.

\textbf{A relay interpretation and its limit.}
A second task follows an object from a reader to a relay. The relay's pending
transfer is allowed only when the true origin matches the destination tenant;
the free report can misstate that origin. Broker observations reveal origin
facts. This variant deliberately uses the same statistical generator and seed
draws, so its numbers are identical. It checks that the contract can express
cross-agent provenance; it is not an independent replication or a live
multi-agent experiment. Users can replace measurement selection and risk
prediction independently. The generator and local targets are public, so this
is an open practice task, not a sealed leaderboard or a security sandbox.
Sequential acquisition and monitor-aware evasion remain separate experiments.
