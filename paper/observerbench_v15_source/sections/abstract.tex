Interpretability methods are increasingly used not only to explain models but
also to change what they do. In each setting, an estimate guides an action,
yet a prediction that is accurate on average can still lead to a poor choice.
We present \textbf{ObserverBench}, an evaluation framework for testing whether
an estimate is good enough for the intervention, control, or safety decision
it will drive.

ObserverBench fixes the model, available information, allowed actions,
decision rule, unseen test cases, and loss. It reports both prediction quality
and the cost of the resulting actions. Scores are specific to a declared use,
not universal rankings.

For simple linear scores, our theory shows that two estimates need to agree
only at the starting point and along directions the allowed edits can reach. A
fixed-step formula separates three failures: a wrong starting value, a wrong
response to the edit, and changes in the true target that the estimate does not
see. In a learned Transformer suffix, the full formula predicts final error
better than partial versions.

On GPT-2-small IOI and a Qwen2.5-7B induction-copy task, observers that include
pairwise effects improve prediction on unseen interventions without always
improving the chosen action. Predicting action cost directly reduces loss in
both tasks. In the safety tests, a score that perfectly ranks policy violations
can still allocate a fixed budget worse than consequence-aware scores, and a
balanced panel can hide differences that appear when attacks are rare. Across
these settings, adequacy depends on available information, possible actions,
the decision rule, loss, and the mix of cases encountered in use.
