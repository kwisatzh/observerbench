\section{Introduction}

Interpretability methods are increasingly used to steer and govern model
behavior rather than merely explain it. Internal estimates now guide concrete
decisions: which activation-steering direction to apply, which attention heads
to remove, whether to permit a risky tool call, or which requests receive a
limited human-review budget. Activation engineering and feedback-control
methods make these estimates part of an active control loop
\cite{turner2023activation,zou2023representation,nguyen2025feedback,skifstad2026local}.
Once an internal estimate is used to choose an action, statistical accuracy is
not a complete evaluation metric. An estimate can perform well on average and
still cause costly errors on individual prompts.

To analyze these systems, we separate the control loop into three components: \textbf{Observer.} The method or probe that reads measurements and
  estimates an internal state or intervention effect \textbf{Controller.} The decision rule that uses the estimate to
  select an action and \textbf{Intervention.} The edit, ablation, or policy action that the
  controller is allowed to execute. ObserverBench asks a central question: is a proposed observer adequate for the
specific decision the controller must make?

Standard prediction metrics do not by themselves show whether an internal
estimate is useful for a decision
\cite{donti2017task,elmachtoub2022smart,orgad2026actionable}. There are three failure
modes that  illustrate why prediction accuracy and decision quality must be reported
separately; (i)  \textbf{Consequence disregard.} In safety triage, an observer can
  classify policy violations perfectly yet spend a limited review budget on
  low-stakes violations, such as unauthorized reads, while allowing
  higher-impact harms, such as unauthorized deletions, through
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:safety}), (ii) \textbf{Dispersion blindness.} In model editing, an observer can
  accurately predict the mean effect of ablating a circuit across a dataset
  yet choose an edit whose effect varies widely across prompts, defeating
  prompt-level control (Sec.~\ref{sec:ioi}), and finally (iii)  \textbf{Redundant signals.} An internal activation probe can achieve
  high predictive accuracy while repeating information already visible in the
  prompt or output logits. It then requires additional model access without
  improving the downstream decision (Sec.~\ref{sec:safety}).


We introduce \textbf{ObserverBench}\footnote{Public workbench and submission
page: \url{https://kwisatzh.github.io/observerbench/}.},
a set of fixed, reusable tasks that makes these operational distinctions
measurable. Each task pins the model, information available to the observer,
allowed interventions, controller, measurement budget, held-out test cases,
and loss. Every observer faces the same controller and evaluation harness
within a task. The evaluator reports two outcomes separately: how accurately
the observer predicts its target and how much loss its chosen action incurs on
held-out cases.

ObserverBench focuses on the operational stage of interpretability.
Mechanistic tomography addresses the measurement problem: how to design probes
and interventions that recover hidden internal mechanisms and effect maps.
ObserverBench addresses the decision problem: whether a proposed estimate is
adequate to guide a particular intervention, control loop, or safety policy
\cite{erramilli2026tomography}.

For safety tasks, ObserverBench freezes the audit policy and compares the
monitor inside it. AI-control evaluations ask the complementary, harder
question of whether the complete protocol remains safe when an adaptive model
tries to subvert it \cite{greenblatt2024aicontrol}. A monitor that performs
well here must still pass that end-to-end test.

An evaluation in ObserverBench is defined by five explicit choices:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Target.} What must the observer estimate: an internal state,
  the average effect of an intervention, or the loss of one action on one
  prompt?

  \item \textbf{Information and integrity boundary.} What may the observer
  read, and who can alter, omit, delay, or spoof those measurements? Examples
  include prompt text, internal activations, output scores, and external logs.

  \item \textbf{Action space.} Which interventions may the controller apply,
  such as a specified activation shift or one mask from a fixed set of
  ablations?

  \item \textbf{Decision rule and loss.} How does the controller turn an
  estimate into an action, and which downstream errors incur a cost?

  \item \textbf{Operating regime.} Which prompts, intervention budgets, attack
  frequencies, episode horizons, and attacker assumptions will the evaluator
  use?
\end{itemize}

These choices make observer adequacy task-relative. Permissions, tool
interfaces, budgets, and attack rates are therefore part of the evaluation,
not background details. In the 2026 OpenAI--Hugging Face incident, agents used
an unsanctioned communication channel, coordinated attempts to fool the
evaluator, and successfully spoofed some tool calls. The case shows why an
information boundary must cover side channels and evaluator-held evidence, not
only prompts and activations
\cite{openai2026securityincident,huggingface2026securityincident,
greenblatt2026hfinvestigation}.

ObserverBench instantiates these choices in three standardized operational
modes---closed-loop control, effect prediction, and safety triage---summarized
in Sec.~\ref{sec:artifact}.

\Needspace{11\baselineskip}
\paragraph{Contributions.}

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.2em,topsep=0.3em]
  \item \textbf{An extensible evaluation workbench.} Researchers can implement
  an observer against a small local interface or submit a prediction table to
  a fixed task. Task and observer cards record what the evaluator fixes and
  what the method may use. Each result reports both prediction quality and the
  cost of the resulting actions (Sec.~\ref{sec:artifact}).

  \item \textbf{A formal adequacy test for control.} For linear readouts with
  an offset, an observer needs to match the target only at the starting point
  and along directions the allowed interventions can reach. An exact
  finite-horizon identity then separates residual reported error,
  starting-state mismatch, and target--observer response mismatch along the
  edit direction (Sec.~\ref{sec:design}, Eq.~\ref{eq:certificate}).

  \item \textbf{Prediction and compression are not decisions.} Pairwise
  models improve held-out effect prediction on GPT-2-small and Qwen2.5-7B, but
  better mean prediction does not reliably improve the chosen GPT-2 action.
  Relative to the same-feature mean observer, direct-loss training reduces
  action loss by 18.5\% in the clean GPT-2 confirmation and by 8.9\% in the
  Qwen aggregate \cite{wang2023ioi,olsson2022induction,qwen2024qwen25}. In the
  Qwen safety task, a sparse SAE readout uses 127
  nonzero coefficients rather than 3,584, yet mean escaped attacks rise from
  2.05 to 4.67: \emph{compression without a better decision}
  (Secs.~\ref{sec:phase7-confirmation}, \ref{sec:qwen-induction}, and
  \ref{sec:safety}) \cite{kantamneni2025saeprobes}. Roughan et al. found the same broader separation in
  traffic engineering: traffic-matrix estimation-error magnitude is not by
  itself a good indicator of routing performance \cite{roughan2003te}.

  \item \textbf{Classification is not enough for safety.} In the authorization
  task, a label observer with AUROC 1.000 allocates a fixed intervention budget
  poorly when it ignores consequence. On the external Gemma APPS task, the
  detailed prompted monitor has higher AUROC than the official Gemma Scope SAE
  probe (0.893 versus 0.870) but more mean realized violations (10.80 versus
  9.07). Across matched Qwen2.5-7B, Gemma-2-9B-it, and prospectively frozen
  Qwen3.5-9B tasks, residual observers lead on average, while the lowest-loss
  context differs---and reverses between the two Qwen checkpoints. On Qwen3.5,
  the official Qwen-Scope SAE probe also incurs more violations than the dense
  residual from which its features are computed; the SAE was trained on the
  base rather than the evaluated post-trained checkpoint
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:safety})
  \cite{greenblatt2024aicontrol,inglis2025controlarena,
  gemmateam2024gemma2,lieberum2024gemmascope}.
\end{itemize}
